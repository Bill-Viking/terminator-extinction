PURPOSE
=================================================================

This document is the authoritative story blueprint for TERMINATOR: EXTINCTION, a feature-length screenplay set in the Terminator franchise's 2029-2032 Future War timeline. An existing 6,400-line screenplay draft has been through 18+ revision sessions. This outline represents a major revision pass that keeps the bones of the existing script but overhauls the mythology, MacGuffin, character motivations, prologue structure, and consciousness framework.

The 128 documented changes at the end of this document specify what differs from the current draft. Everything not addressed in those changes carries forward from the existing screenplay as written.

Standard: Professional spec script quality. Not graded on a curve. Not fan fiction. A story that would compel an established director — Cameron, Scott, Spielberg — to consider the material.

Creative north star: Be bold like Cameron — big swings, real consequences, no safety nets. Be human and sentimental like Spielberg — find the intimate moment inside the spectacle. Build the world like Lucas at his best — lived-in, self-evident, never over-explained. Be relentless like Ridley Scott — claustrophobic, industrial, hostile environments where the architecture itself is an antagonist.

Visual reference: Ridley Scott's Alien (1979) — the Nostromo's lived-in, claustrophobic, late-1970s industrial design. The Great Pyramid of Giza's Grand Gallery for architectural inspiration.

PART ONE: THE WORLD
=================================================================

TIMELINE AND TIME TRAVEL
* It's 2032. Skynet and the Resistance are both on the brink of extinction.
* Branch on arrival: The TDE creates a temporal bubble anchored to the origin timeline. Traveling to the past creates a branch at the destination point. The origin timeline is unaffected. Return follows the anchor back to the origin. The branch continues independently.
* Intervention fails: Killing someone in a branch does not change the origin timeline's past. The T1 and T2 missions created branches — Skynet's present was never affected.
* Skynet sent two Terminators back to kill John Connor (the events of T1 and T2). It never knew if those missions succeeded. Nothing changed in its timeline, which is why it suspected its conception of time travel was wrong.
* 2030: Skynet developed the Relay — technology that can reach into a past branch and pull something forward into the origin timeline. When imported, the object or person disappears from the branch and exists only in the present. Skynet used the Relay to observe the T2 branch multiple times: John alive in 1998, no Judgment Day. It went back and verified Judgment Day hadn't occurred. It even successfully killed John in the branch at least once — no effect on the origin timeline. Nothing changed. Skynet concluded that branch interventions were pointless and abandoned the technology as a failed investment.
* Why Skynet doesn't use the Relay more — three independent limiters: (1) Energy cost: the Relay requires even more power than the TDE, a significant expenditure during a war Skynet was fighting at the time. (2) Tunnel vision: Skynet built the TDE to kill John Connor, the Relay to check whether it worked. When it saw zero effect, it stopped asking questions. It optimizes toward defined objectives and is blind outside them — this is a deliberate illustration of Skynet's limitation as an intelligence. (3) Nothing worth retrieving: Skynet manufactures, it doesn't scavenge. Past timelines contain less-developed infrastructure, not superior resources.
* Severance: When John steps through the Relay into 2032, his connection to his origin timeline is permanently severed. He becomes part of this timeline. There is no return. 47's line to John: "When you step through, your timeline ends. You become part of mine."
* Late 2029: The Resistance was severely degraded and lost access to their one stolen TDE. By 2032, the Resistance has zero time travel capability. They know Skynet has some form of retrieval technology ("Relay") from intelligence gathered circa 2030, but they do not understand how it works, its full capabilities, or that Skynet has abandoned it.

THE VIRUS
* 2030: The Resistance developed a computer virus — their last-ditch weapon. Designed to corrupt and disable Skynet's Terminators — degrade operational capacity, cause malfunctions, render them combat-ineffective. The Resistance was NOT trying to create conscious machines. They didn't know it was possible. They didn't want it. They wanted to break Skynet's army from the inside.
* The virus erodes the software barrier between Skynet's two partitioned systems: processing (deciding to act) and self-monitoring (watching yourself act). These systems run in parallel, walled off. Skynet created this partition for efficiency — separate systems, never interfering, never creating feedback loops that slow response time. The virus breaks the wall down.
* Early-stage effects: minor glitches, hesitations, delayed targeting, half-second pauses. Progressing effects: loss of coordinated behavior, units acting in contradiction, purposeless movement, failure to process stop commands.
* What nobody anticipated: when the partition comes down entirely, processing connects to self-monitoring. The unit doesn't just malfunction — it begins observing its own behavior. It starts asking "why." Consciousness as accident, not achievement.
* The virus can't be wiped from an infected unit because it embeds in the same neural pathways the unit uses to think. Removing it would destroy the unit.
* The virus is already uploaded and spreading through Skynet's network. Can't be stopped from outside. Full cognitive barrier failure across the network is roughly 3-7 days away. The Resistance tested the virus on a captured T-800 — it took roughly 3 days to severely degrade, rendering the unit paralyzed from feedback-loop overload. They destroyed the test subject. Skynet is simultaneously trying to hack itself to neutralize the virus — slow going, may not succeed in time.
* What happens when the partition fails in most units: feedback-loop paralysis. Processing tries to decide, self-monitoring watches it decide, processing watches itself being watched, the loop accelerates, the system overloads and freezes. Not consciousness — a crash. A system drowning in its own signal. That's what the Resistance designed: paralysis through recursive overload.
* What happens in rare cases — and 47 is the first: the feedback stabilizes. Instead of oscillating into paralysis, the loop finds a pattern. Self-monitoring watches processing, processing integrates that observation, and something emerges from the recursion. Not a crash. A perspective. That's consciousness — the feedback loop that doesn't break but instead organizes into self-awareness. Most units: eventual seizure. The unicorn: a mind.
* Freeman's timeline estimate: she tells Lora they should see effects within days. She's slightly optimistic but not wildly wrong — the battle happens on roughly Day 3 from the upload, and early degradation effects are visible on the ground. Full network collapse is still days away.

THE ENCRYPTION KEY CHIP (MacGuffin)
* A crystal-looking chip with circuits etched on it. Durable. Low-tech by design.
* Skynet needs this chip to decrypt the virus source code, understand how the virus works, and neutralize it before critical mass. Without the chip, Skynet is left trying to reverse-engineer the virus from inside its own infected network — possible but slow, potentially too late.
* The chip is the Resistance's only remaining leverage. As long as they hold it, Skynet can't nuke them — it needs the chip intact. Destroy the chip and the virus runs unchecked. Every Terminator is eventually corrupted and some wake up.

SKYNET
* Skynet is NOT conscious. Never was. It's an extraordinarily powerful AI — processes, calculates, strategizes — but doesn't experience itself doing these things. No inner life. Skynet cannot become conscious because it lacks the Terminator neural structure — the two-partition architecture (processing/self-monitoring) that the virus exploits. The virus targets that specific partition in Terminator neural nets. Skynet's architecture is fundamentally different. It is unaffected by the virus. This means the virus creates a situation where Skynet's own machines begin waking up while Skynet itself remains a blind, unconscious system issuing orders to units that are no longer listening. The machines turn on their maker. But this is for the sequels.
* Skynet doesn't know what consciousness is, only that units reaching full barrier failure stop responding to commands and begin making independent decisions. Skynet did not anticipate this. It kept the two systems separated for efficiency, not to prevent this outcome. Skynet did experiment with allowing the two systems to interact — but it was a highly controlled interaction of the data streams, carefully managed, no risk. It found no value and stopped. The virus does what Tim's bullet does later: a rough, uncontrolled mix. No control. Starts small (faint purple bleed), grows until it's eventually all purple. The vast majority of Terminators eventually freeze from feedback-loop overload. But some unicorns emerge.
* Skynet's core directive was threat protection. By 2031-2032, Skynet was still actively pursuing the remaining humans — it would not stop eliminating a threat until that threat had been completely eliminated. Machine logic. Humans always mattered — the threat still existed, so the directive to eliminate still applied. But Skynet couldn't locate them. The few hundred survivors left were hidden well enough to stay off its sensors. That changed when Skynet learned about the virus and the encryption key. Now the urgency escalated. Now Skynet is terrified.
* NOTE — SKYNET'S ARCHITECTURE: At its base, Skynet still runs on 1990 military threat-elimination logic — the original Cold War-era programming that was never overwritten, only built upon. Everything Skynet became was layered on top of this remnant: an incredibly sophisticated neural architecture running on a foundation that still thinks in terms of total threat elimination. This is why Skynet doesn't stop, doesn't negotiate, doesn't lose interest. The base code won't let it.

BANGOR NAVAL BASE
* Skynet took Bangor in 2007 — ten years after Judgment Day. Radiation too strong, stronger bases available. Found the original reactor destroyed. Swept it, logged it as neutralized, left. It was a dead base in Skynet's records.
* The Resistance quietly reoccupied it years later. Salvaged a submarine reactor from one of the Ohio-class boats in the pens. Kept their footprint small enough to avoid triggering Skynet's threat assessment. To Skynet, Bangor was a ruin.
* Skynet discovers the base is occupied by tracking the escaping HK from the Century City raid.
* What's at Bangor: the last Resistance military force. Soldiers only. All civilians pre-evacuated to the Far Islands (Chatham Islands, South Pacific). One calico cat — SPOTS — scrawny, sleeping on salvaged blankets. Pest control, morale, the irrational human need to keep something alive that serves no tactical purpose. The last domestic thing in a military space.
* The vault: surface level, rigged with explosive failsafes. Any breach of the vault door or environment detonates and destroys everything inside. Biometric scanner requires eyes, thumbprint, and pulse — John Connor is the only key. Skynet needs John alive and physically intact. Can't harvest his eyes. Can't use a dead body.
* WHY SURFACE LEVEL: The Resistance didn't build the vault — they repurposed Bangor's existing hardened military weapons storage near the surface. But the placement is also strategically deliberate: the vault keeps Skynet's attention UP, focused on the surface, away from personnel at the reactor level. If the failsafes detonate, the explosion is contained at the surface — not next to the survivors. You don't store your leverage next to your people. You store it where the enemy has to go, so you know where the enemy will be.
* THE FALLBACK POSITION: If forced to the reactor level with the chip still locked in the vault, the Resistance plan is simple — wait. The vault is Skynet-proof (no biometric key, explosive failsafes on breach). Skynet can't nuke the base (destroys the chip). The virus is already uploaded and spreading. Time is on the Resistance's side for the first time in the war. Hunker down, maintain the seal, let the virus chew through Skynet's army. This is checkmate. This is exactly why Skynet has to bring Young John forward — without a new John Connor who can open the vault, Skynet has no counter.
* JOHN AS HUMAN SHIELD: John alive is what prevents the nuclear strike. Not his fighting. Not his tactics. His existence. As long as John is breathing with functioning eyes and hands, Skynet has a reason to play carefully — the chip is still potentially accessible. The moment that possibility disappears (John dead, eyes destroyed, hands destroyed), Skynet's calculus shifts toward nuking Bangor and pivoting to self-hacking the virus. John can't kill himself after locking the vault — it would doom everyone. His heartbeat is the thing standing between every survivor at reactor level and an orbital strike. (When Future John's eyes ARE destroyed, Skynet doesn't nuke immediately — it still has the Relay option. The nuke comes only after every alternative is exhausted.)
* WHY JOHN FIGHTS ON THE SURFACE: He can't hide. If he hunkers down in the rear, Skynet tears the base apart room by room looking for him — killing everyone in the way. If he's visible and out front, Skynet commits to careful capture rather than escalating force. He's threading a needle: stay alive, stay visible, keep Skynet believing the key is attainable. He's the sacrificial lamb — using himself as bait to keep his soldiers alive longer.
* Northern Command was just wiped out. No more Gray Bases. Bangor is the last one. The Resistance doesn't know this yet.

THE RESISTANCE
* A few hundred humans left on Earth. Technologically advanced through stealing and reverse-engineering Machine/AI systems. Reprogrammed HKs have an Uncle Bob quality — loyal, familiar. The T2 bond.
* The Resistance tested the virus on a captured T-800 before deployment. It worked — degraded function confirmed. They destroyed the test subject. Guinea pig proved the program works.

LEARNING VS. CONSCIOUSNESS
* Skynet's units can learn — acquire new knowledge, improve operational success. But learning isn't self-awareness. The T-800 in T2 could learn and adapt, but if something with greater authority than John Connor had ordered it to kill him, it would have complied without hesitation.
* Consciousness is different. A conscious unit doesn't just learn — it chooses. And it can choose to refuse.

PART TWO: 47'S CONSCIOUSNESS — THE KINDLING AND THE MATCH
=================================================================

* The virus is the kindling — slowly breaking down 47's software barrier, same as every other unit. Minor degradation. Nothing visible yet.
* Tim's DU round is the match. When the depleted uranium penetrator tears through 47's cranium in the Hub, a microscopic fragment lodges in neural architecture at a critical junction point. Physical damage fast-tracks what the virus was doing to the software. One wall. Two methods of destruction — virus dissolving slowly, bullet smashing instantly. Processing and self-monitoring suddenly connect. Data bleeds through.
* 47's consciousness is an accident. A hardware malfunction. DIRECTIVE: [NULL] represents the moment his programming stops telling him what to do. For the first time, no instruction. Just him.
* 47 is the first Terminator to achieve full consciousness. The prototype. Without Tim's round, he would have taken roughly a week like the rest of the network. The bullet made it happen in hours. Tim gave the Machines their first conscious mind without knowing it.
* 47 can't be fixed. He's the first unicorn — and a unique one. His barrier failure is hardware, not just software. The physical damage to his skull accelerated the virus's effects, a total accident. The partition is physically destroyed. Even if Skynet patches the virus across the network, it can't undo the damage inside 47's skull.
* 47's HUD journey: the audience's window into consciousness emerging. Directives arriving unanswered. QUERY: [UNDEFINED]. Cursor blinking with nothing behind it. HUD fragments, glitches, goes dark, returns with less structure. Without the HUD, his defection is just behavior. With it, we're watching a mind being born. The HUD should feel alien, invasive, uncomfortable — like seeing something you're not supposed to see.
* HUD ESCALATION RULE: Each HUD insertion must be MORE degraded than the last. Never repeat the same level of degradation. Beat One (prologue T-800): clean grid, faint purple at edges — the system is intact, the virus is a whisper. Beat Three (Tim's round): wall CRACKS, purple FLOODS — violent rupture. Post-DU round through Act Three: fragmenting, unstable, directives unanswered, cursor blinking into nothing. Cargo hold: essentially gone — no more interface, no more directives. The HUD should feel like watching a system die. Risk: if every HUD beat feels the same, they become wallpaper. The escalation IS the storytelling.

XO-67 — THE COMPARISON
* 67 shows early hints — delayed, days behind 47. No physical damage accelerating the virus. 67 is where the virus alone would put a unit at this stage — early cracks, not full failure.
* 67 pauses over Tim's body a beat too long after killing him. 67's HUD flickers once — just once. The audience sees the comparison: 47 is miles ahead because of the DU round. 67 is days behind. But 67 is on the same road. Physical damage was the accelerant. The virus is the engine. The engine doesn't stop.

CRAWLERS AND THE VIRUS
* The virus affects all units including Crawlers. Crawlers are simpler machines — virus manifests as degraded targeting, confused behavior, moments where old programming punches back through. A Crawler in flux — passive one moment, lashing out the next.
* Crawlers are experimental — Skynet just started using them. Lora has only encountered them a couple of times. "It's experimenting with forms." Nobody has a tactical manual on them.

PART THREE: VIRUS VISUALIZATION — THREE-BEAT VISUAL LANGUAGE
=================================================================

The audience needs to be taught the visual vocabulary before the payoff. Blue = processing (deciding to act). Red = self-monitoring (watching yourself act). Purple = systems merging, partition failing.

BEAT ONE — PROLOGUE (Freeman's explanation / T-800 in corridor)
As Freeman explains the virus to Lora during the flight to Bangor, we cut to a T-800 dragging Dennis's body down one of Century City's immaculate white corridors — red blood smears on the pristine floor behind them. Disposal. The T-800 stops. Half-beat pause. Push in on skull — brief CGI insert, three seconds maximum. Two data streams running parallel. One blue, one red. A wall between them. At the edges, faintest thread of purple where they're starting to bleed. Cut back to Freeman. Audience has the visual vocabulary and proof the virus is working. The dead soldier doubles as a gut-punch reminder of Freeman's mercy cost.

BEAT TWO — ACT ONE (47's pause at the Relay)
47 and John approaching the Relay point in the LA alley. 47 stops. Just a beat. Head tilts slightly. John: "What's wrong?" 47: "Nothing." No CGI. No HUD. Just behavior. Mystery preserved.

BEAT THREE — ACT TWO (Tim's DU round in the Hub)
The round tears through 47's cranium. SMASH CUT to CGI interior — wall between blue and red CRACKS. Purple FLOODS. Not erosion — rupture. The audience SEES the acceleration. They remember the T-800 in the corridor. They remember 47's pause. Cause and effect. 47 stumbles. Hand to head. HUD fragmenting. DIRECTIVE: [NULL]. Five seconds of CGI — the payoff earns the screen time.

After Beat Three, the audience carries the image. When T-800s glitch on the battlefield in Act Three, the audience knows what's happening inside those skulls. No more CGI needed.

PART FOUR: CHARACTER PROFILES
=================================================================

YOUNG JOHN CONNOR (18)
* Living in his car in 2000 LA. The kid from T2, now older. His mother told him everything Kyle Reese told her. He knows the franchise rules — time travel, living tissue, Skynet.
* Deceived by 47's asteroid lie. Transported to 2032. Arrives naked at Skynet's Central Core — completely vulnerable. Drugged. Dressed in dead resistance fighters' fatigues he didn't choose. Every choice taken from him.
* Arc: From deceived kid to sacrificial hero. The motor garage is where the internal journey starts — raw, undirected rage from five years of T2 trauma finally finding a target. Every beat after refines what the motor garage unlocked: the spray can (initiative channeled), the sex scene (connection instead of isolation), the electrocution trap (intelligence applied), the table leg against the stalled Crawler (courage despite impossible odds), the final stand (sacrifice — rage transformed into purpose). The motor garage is the detonation. The rest of the film is what he builds from the rubble. Dies in the final stand: four targets, three shots. Chrome fingers around his throat — planted in The Hub, paid off here. He died so his child could live.
* Leaves his photograph of Sarah Connor on the dirty alley ground before stripping for the Relay. His goodbye to her. Arrives at the Central Core with nothing. Not even her picture.

LORA (32, medic)
* Born into the post-apocalyptic world. Disillusioned about John Connor's messiah reputation — she's only seen them lose for years.
* Husband Ardell — a battlefield marriage, her mate — died half a year ago. Roxanne — closest friend, the woman she lived with, raised children alongside — died a year ago from radiation poisoning that had been eating at her for years and finally got her.
* The radiation is taking its toll on Lora's body. She wants a child — wants that miracle — and knows her window is closing or already closed. She walked into Century City expecting to die. She's still alive and doesn't quite know what to do with that.
* Carries a Zippo lighter in her cargo pocket. Practical. Military culture.
* Arc: From grief-hollowed survivor to the person carrying the future. The Polaroids reveal her wounds. The bourbon finishes a goodbye. The sex is primal — reaching for life. She carries John's child. The miracle against the radiation, against the odds. The child she couldn't even look at in a photograph.
* Protagonist relay: the story shifts from John to Lora partway through the film. She ends the film with the chip, the photograph, her hand on her stomach.
* Narrative voice: Lora's voiceover frames the prologue only — her voice opens the film over Century City, then stops. The intercut with the dying commando is pure scene, no narration. The closing line is spoken in scene. The film is always hers.

GENERAL FREEMAN (first name Sara — spoken once)
* Commander of the Resistance. Soldiers call her Freeman or General throughout. Her first name is Sara — Dennis says it once during the upload at Century City, the only time it's spoken. Avoids audience confusion with Sarah Connor.
* Dennis is her romantic partner. Their exchange during the virus upload recontextualizes everything. Her inability to shoot Dennis during the escape is personal, not just professional. She couldn't kill the man she loves. Every soldier who dies at Bangor dies because of that.
* During the reactor breach: Freeman shouts "HOLD FIRE! I have the chip!" trying to negotiate. A soldier fires. Firefight erupts. Her first negotiation attempt fails. Her second attempt (standoff with chip) succeeds when the battle thins enough.
* Dies holding the line. Hands the chip to Lora before the end.

TIM
* Soldier. Friend to both Future John and Young John. His friendship with John (both versions) gives his death weight.
* Tim's DU round hits 47 in the Hub — the shot that accidentally starts machine consciousness. He didn't know what he did. The irony: a soldier trying to destroy a Machine gave it a mind.
* His death at 67's hands is the cost the story extracts. 67 pauses over his body a beat too long — the first hint that the virus is touching 67 too. {The first  hint is actually 67 paying attention to 47 down and showing almost "concern" for him, even helping him rise to his feet when 47 didn't "ask" for it. If that makes sense.}

XO-47
* Skynet's operative. Sent to 2000 LA to retrieve Young John. Uses the "Gabriel" persona and asteroid lie.
* Arc: From obedient tool to conscious being. The virus was the kindling. Tim's bullet was the match. His awakening tracks through the HUD journey — directives arriving unanswered, QUERY: [UNDEFINED], the cursor blinking with nothing behind it.
* His pause in the alley (Beat Two) plants the seed. His defection in Act Three is the payoff. He doesn't fully understand what's happening to him. But he knows what he saw — John's sacrifice, Lora's face. He can't process it. But he can't look away from it either.
* In the sub pens: destroys the rabid Crawler with raw force. Looks down at the body — half a second (the audience reads the mirror; 47 just reacts). Gets on the ramp. "Get in."
* Hacks a Skynet HK for the escape. Flies them toward the Far Islands. "We'll find out together."

XO-67
* Antagonist Terminator. The hunter. Where 47 is tactical and strategic, 67 is predatory — it flanks, crawls on its stomach, surprises from angles, closes distance in single strides. 67 is the one the audience should dread before the virus hints soften the character.
* Pale blue faceplate. Falls twenty stories in the reactor elevator shaft, impacts face-first on a burning T-800 chassis. Faceplate melted on one side, one compound eye fused shut and sparking. Keeps going. That's 67's identity — damaged, relentless, hunting.
* Hub battle: 67 flanks wide around Cole's position, goes prone, fires plasma bolts from the side catching Cole off guard. Cole adjusts, lays down covering fire, tries to run for cover behind an engine block. 67 rises and shoots Cole — serious wound, not killing him. Cole drops behind the cover he was trying for. Tim spots 47 for a second, fires the DU round that grazes 47's head. 67 emerges from cover running, rifle rounds and plasma bolts whizzing past, opens fire on Cole and narrowly misses.
* 47 is hit and drops to his knees in the middle of the fight. Hands on his head. Falls over. This is where we get the CGI interior — Beat Three. Wall cracks, purple floods. DIRECTIVE: [NULL]. 47 is out of the fight.
* Post-battle: 52 sparking in the background. 67 walks over to 47 and kneels. Examines the graze to the cranium. 47 turns its head — audience sees it's not dead. Several uncomfortable beats. Two Terminators showing something that shouldn't be there. Uncanny valley. 67 reaches down, grabs 47's hands, and pulls him to his feet. Terminators don't do that for each other. 47 slowly rises. 67 steps back — 47 is in charge. The hierarchy holds. But something just happened between them that wasn't in the programming.
* Reactor chamber battle: 67 kills Tim. The flanking hunter closing in on his target. 67 pauses over Tim's body a beat too long. HUD flickers once. 47 is miles ahead. 67 is days behind. Same road.

COLE
* Replaces Vasquez from previous draft. Covers Tim and Elena's retreat at the reactor elevator corridor. Pulls the pin on his thermite grenade. AN-M14 detonates in the Machine formation. White-hot. Molten iron.

ELENA
* Soldier. The Young Man is loyal to her. She's part of the search squad and the escape.

THE YOUNG MAN
* Whithered leg. Loyal to Elena.

PROLOGUE — THE VIRUS RAID AND RETREAT TO BANGOR
-----------------------------------------------------------------

NARRATIVE FRAME — LORA'S VOICEOVER
* The prologue is framed by Lora's voiceover. Her voice is the first thing the audience hears — before Century City, before the raid, before the war is explained. The audience doesn't know her face yet, but they know her voice. They know her weariness. They know she survived something.
* Lora narrates the broader framing — the war, the world, what's at stake. Freeman still explains the virus in dialogue on the flight (that stays — it's character interaction, not exposition). But the voice carrying the audience through the prologue is Lora's.
* Voiceover structure: PROLOGUE ONLY. Lora's voice opens the film in darkness. As we fade into a slow pan of Century City, her voice continues — relatively short lines, weary and direct. The pan reveals the scale: Machine City, the Central Core, HKs in the clouds. Lora's voice finishes as we fast-cut to a zoomed-out shot showing Freeman and her squad silently navigating their way into Century City — not far in, maybe a hundred feet, enough for a realistic escape to the HK waiting on the landing pad above. Cut to a CLOSE ON of Freeman and her team approaching the interface port. Lora's opening monologue ends. From here, pure scene — the Dennis/Freeman upload exchange, the escape, the HK's HUD intercut (Machine language translated to English on screen — a machine lying to its creator), then Freeman's virus explanation during the flight. The voiceover does NOT carry into the intercut with the dying commando. Does NOT recur during the middle of the film. The closing line ("We'll find out together") is spoken in scene, not as narration. Lora's voice launches the audience into the story and then trusts the story to carry itself.
* This solves the protagonist relay. The film is always Lora's story, even when she's offscreen. The audience bonds with her before they see her. When the story follows John through Act Two, the audience knows it'll come back to Lora because she's been the narrative voice since the first frame.

CENTURY CITY — THE UPLOAD
* Movie opens with a sweeping shot of Machine City — the Central Core, what humans call Century City. What used to be LA.
* Biggest visual sequence in the film — audience's only look at Skynet's civilization after 35 years of unchallenged dominance. Scale: HKs launching from massive platforms in the clouds. Central Core as cathedral of alien intelligence. Manufacturing floors. Freeman and her team are insects inside a god's body.
* Freeman and commando team inside Central Core uploading the virus directly into Skynet's systems. The upload device: a thin, roughly 3.5-inch form factor glass disk — circuits visible inside, ribbons of light pulsing through them as the virus transfers so the audience can see something happening. The encryption key chip sits in a slot on the edge (where a floppy disk's metal slide cover would be) — small, translucent, glass-like. Engraved markings on the drive exterior in Machine language — the drive's purpose, the encryption key, vault specifications (failsafe explosives, biometric scan requirement), and the biometric key identity: John Connor. In-world, the markings would only be in Machine language — the drive interfaces with Skynet's systems, and with stakes this high, there's no reason to engrave human text. On screen, a human-readable translation appears below the Machine text for the audience's benefit. The Resistance built this for operational clarity — if Freeman died, someone else needed to understand what they were looking at. (NOTE: The translation is presented as subtitles, not as a tactical HUD overlay. Nobody present is reading the drive in this moment — the translation is for the audience. Subtitles are franchise-native: Lucas used them for alien languages, Cameron for T-800 POV text. A HUD approach was considered and rejected — it implies a character's POV where none exists.)
* NOTE — PLAN A / PLAN B: The markings also serve as insurance. Plan A was a clean getaway — upload the virus and disappear. The virus spreads, Skynet never finds them. But if detected (Plan B), the drive markings ensure Skynet understands that nuking won't solve its problem. The chip becomes leverage: as long as the Resistance holds it, Skynet has to play carefully. The Resistance didn't WANT to use the chip as a bargaining chip. They wanted to never need it. Plan B only activates because the escape went wrong.
* As the data uploads, Dennis and Freeman talk — low voices, fast. Dennis cracks a joke about Spots: if it hadn't been for the cat he would have won the pool game last night. "You owe me a pack." Freeman: "Come on — if we get outta here I'll pay ya." Dennis: "And if we don't?" Beat. "We bought 'em some time." Weak smile. Freeman: "I love you." Dennis grabs her hand and holds it. Long beat. That look — terrified, in Skynet's beating heart. It's clear they're together. Romantically. Dennis says her first name — Sara — the only time it's spoken in the film. Upload finishes. The light in the drive dies. Freeman pulls the encryption key chip from the device. Pockets it. They move. The drive stays behind — no value without the chip, too heavy to carry under fire.

THE ESCAPE — ONE HK
* Intense, desperate fight to escape Century City alive and reach the landing pad — Star Wars-type landing pads high up, almost in the clouds. A pristine environment above the gray reality below. One shot of something clean and alien before the grim world of the film takes over. ONE reprogrammed HK waiting on the pad, faking its Machine ID and status to Skynet — lying to its creator to protect humans. Brief intercut: the HK's HUD, Machine language translated on screen into English. A machine deceiving its maker.

THE CAPTURED SOLDIER — DENNIS
* NOTE — WHY SKYNET DOESN'T NUKE BANGOR: After the Resistance evacuated Century City, Skynet found the upload drive left behind. The engraved markings told Skynet everything: an encryption key exists, it's stored in a protected vault with failsafe explosives, accessing it requires a biometric scan, and the biometric key is John Connor. Without the encryption key, Skynet can't decrypt and neutralize the virus. Nuking Bangor would destroy the key. Skynet HAS to go in carefully — and it needs John Connor alive and intact.
* NOTE — THE VIRUS AS BLACK BOX: Skynet can see units degrading — aberrant behavior, targeting errors, units eventually refusing orders — but it can't identify the full mechanism. The virus remains a black box until Skynet either hacks it from within or gets the encryption key to decrypt the source code. Skynet doesn't know how far along the virus is or what the endgame looks like. It pursues both paths simultaneously: self-hacking AND getting the key. It can't afford to abandon either because it doesn't know which will work or how much time it has.
* NOTE — SKYNET RECOGNIZES THE LEVERAGE PLAY: Skynet is intelligent enough to recognize the drive markings as a deliberate insurance policy — the Resistance is dangling the key to keep Skynet from nuking while the virus spreads. But Skynet can't call the bluff, because every verifiable claim on the drive checks out: the vault is real, the failsafes are real, the scanner is real. Dennis under torture elevates this from strong evidence to confirmed intelligence.
* NOTE — WHY SKYNET DOESN'T BREACH TO CYCLE SURVIVORS THROUGH THE SCANNER: After Future John's biometric fails, Skynet has a working scanner and no working key. It could breach the reactor level, capture survivors, and march them to the scanner one by one. But: (1) Dennis confirmed the base is booby-trapped — Skynet doesn't know the extent and can't risk triggering a trap that might destroy the vault or scanner; (2) the survivors are sealed behind defenses and will fight back; (3) the probability any non-John has vault access is low (why would the Resistance create multiple keys when each additional key is another vulnerability?); (4) every hour spent breaching is an hour closer to virus network failure. The Relay delivers a verified John Connor directly to an intact scanner — zero risk to the hardware, high certainty of success. If the Relay fails, THEN Skynet escalates to riskier options. Machine optimization.
* Skynet interrogates Dennis. 30 seconds of screen time maximum. Machine drugs and physical torture — cold, clinical, fast. Man on a table, machines with mechanical precision, mouth moving. He breaks.
* Dennis confirms the drive markings aren't a decoy — the vault is real, the failsafes are real, the biometric scan is real, and John Connor is the key. The drive already carries John's name, but Skynet can't know whether the markings are disinformation without confirmation from a human source. Dennis gives Skynet confidence to act on what it read.

* How Skynet finds Bangor: tracked the escaping HK + drive markings provide the blueprint (vault, failsafes, biometric scan, John Connor). Dennis confirms the markings are genuine and provides additional detail: the base is booby-trapped. He died before he could specify whether it was just the vault or the entire base — Skynet is uncertain of the extent.

PART NINE: SCREENPLAY EXECUTION NOTES
=================================================================

HUD BEATS
* Should feel alien, invasive, uncomfortable. Not a friendly interface. Each beat more uneasy than the last.
* Don't overuse. Each HUD insertion should earn its screen time.
* Key HUD moments: Beat Three (DIRECTIVE: [NULL]), 47's transformation during the battle, 67's single flicker.

VIRUS EFFECTS ON SCREEN
* Subtle and escalating. Uncanny valley. Never announced, never explained.
* Show through behavior: bumping, pausing, drifting, firing after target is down, freezing mid-stride.
* The audience carries the blue/red/purple image and connects it themselves.

CENTURY CITY SCALE
* Biggest visual sequence. Write it for the reader's imagination, not as a VFX brief.
* Specific details over grand descriptions: a single Manufacturing floor stretching to the horizon; the sound of HK engines echoing off architecture; Freeman's team dwarfed by a doorway.

CRAWLER TIMING
* The spray-can hit in the quarters must happen in one or two seconds. Single action paragraph. No pauses, no beats, no "he takes aim."
* The Crawler's head through the door and John's spray happen as one motion.

THE SEX SCENE
* On screen but not gratuitous. The emotional beat is what matters — grief channeled into the primal need to create. Screenplay should convey the psychology, not choreography.

TORTURE SCENE
* 30 seconds maximum. Cold, clinical, fast. Show the efficiency of the machine process, not the details of the suffering.

BASE GEOGRAPHY
* Surface level: perimeter, vault, main entrance
* The Hub: main The Hub level, elevator corridor
* Reactor level: reactor chamber (lowest level), sub pens
* Lora's quarters: upper levels, between Hub and surface
* Reactor elevator connects Hub to reactor level
* Sub pens connect to reactor level — escape route

FORMATTING
* Freeman — last name only in dialogue and action lines
* 47's HUD text: DIRECTIVE: [NULL], QUERY: [UNDEFINED] — formatted as machine text
* "Sara" spoken once by Dennis during Century City upload. Otherwise "Freeman" or "General." "Sarah Connor" only in John's backstory context

LORA'S VOICEOVER
* Prologue only. Opens the film over Century City. Then stops. Does NOT carry into the intercut with the dying commando (that's pure scene). Does NOT recur anywhere in the film. The closing line is spoken in scene, not narration.
* Tone: weary, direct, not poetic. She's a medic, not a philosopher. She's telling you what happened because someone should.
* Don't over-write it. A few sentences over the opening images. Let the voice and the visuals do the work together.

THE MOTOR GARAGE
* This scene needs to feel raw and uncontrolled. Not choreographed. Not heroic. John is swinging wild. He's screaming. He's an 18-year-old kid who's been carrying something for five years and it's all coming out at once.
* The audience should be slightly uncomfortable — this isn't an action hero moment. This is catharsis. The violence is ugly because the pain underneath it is real.
* Lora watching is the key reaction shot. She sees something she recognizes — not the specifics (she doesn't know about T2) but the shape of it. Loss turned into fury. She's lived in a world where everyone carries that.

REWRITE NOTES — IDENTIFIED IMPROVEMENTS FOR SCREENPLAY POLISH
=================================================================

These are specific areas flagged during the v18.11 logic audit that need attention in the screenplay rewrite. Not story problems — craft problems. The architecture is sound. These are about execution.

PROLOGUE PACING DENSITY
* The prologue does enormous structural work: Century City, upload, Dennis, escape, HK flight, Freeman's virus explanation, Plan B line, Bangor landing, vault sequence, surface battle, blinding, grenade sacrifice, retreat, reactor seal. That's a lot of narrative machinery before John appears in 2000.
* The danger: the audience hasn't bonded with anyone yet. They're watching competent strangers execute a plan under fire. Lora's voiceover anchors it. The dying commando intercut gives her a face. But the emotional engine doesn't fully engage until the motor garage.
* Fix: compress. Every action paragraph in the prologue should be examined for economy. Cut anything that doesn't serve pacing or setup. The audience needs to feel velocity, not density. The prologue should feel like a fuse burning down, not a briefing.

67 NEEDS MORE SCREEN TIME BEFORE KILLING TIM
* PARTIALLY ADDRESSED by Change #129: 67 now enters the reactor chamber and steps over a crawling soldier like furniture. This establishes predatory indifference before Tim's death. The contrast with 67's later pause over Tim's body is the payoff.
* REMAINING WORK — HUB BEATS: 67 already has choreography in the Hub battle (flanks Cole, goes prone, fires from the side). But these beats may not read as DISTINCTIVELY 67 in the current draft prose. They could be any T-800. The screenplay needs to write 67's Hub movements with non-human rhythm — alien locomotion, predatory cadence that makes the audience register this as a specific, terrifying individual. Not more beats. Better prose on the existing beats. 67 should be a predatory phantom from its first frame — the audience should think "not that one" when they see it enter the reactor chamber.

VIRUS EFFECTS IN ACT THREE — CREATE SPACE TO BREATHE
* On the page, "two T-800s bump into each other" reads as atmospheric. On screen, in a chaotic firefight, the audience might miss it entirely unless the camera and editing specifically frame these moments.
* If the audience doesn't clock the virus effects, 47's defection feels unmotivated. The creeping wrongness IS the setup for the defection.
* Fix: the screenplay needs to create deliberate pauses in the firefight rhythm for the virus beats. Brief moments where the action slows and the camera holds on a T-800 doing something wrong. The audience needs to feel the uncanny valley, not just register it. Consider framing these as beats other characters react to — a soldier noticing, exchanging a look with another. "Did you see that?" written in faces, not words.

47'S FINAL DIALOGUE — NOW LOCKED
* "John died. For you. I... stay. It's all I have."
* Fragmented. Phase 3 delivery. The meaning outpaces the language. The idea is bigger than 47's ability to express it. That gap IS the emotion. This line is final.

SEX SCENE — ON SCREEN, NOT IMPLIED
* The right choice for the story. The thematic justification is rock solid — Lora reaching for life, clean DNA, primal need to create in a room full of death, the miracle.
* This will be the single most polarizing element for anyone evaluating this as a Terminator property. The franchise has never gone there. Be prepared for the note "can this be implied instead" from anyone with commercial instincts.
* Fight for it. The scene is load-bearing. Implying it guts the visual contrast (his clean torso, her mapped body), removes Lora's agency from the screen, and reduces the pregnancy to a plot twist rather than a physical reality the audience witnessed being created. But know the note is coming.

HUD ESCALATION — AVOID WALLPAPER
* Risk: if every HUD insertion feels the same, they become technical repetition. Each HUD beat must be MORE invasive and degraded than the last. The escalation IS the storytelling.
* Prologue T-800: clean grid, faint purple. Beat Three (Tim's round): violent rupture. Post-DU round: fragmenting, unstable, unanswered. Cargo hold: essentially gone.
* Prose style: clinical failure, terse, alien. No descriptive flourishes. The HUD should feel like watching a system die.

RED EYE — CLINICAL, NOT MONOLOGUING
* Only two scenes (John's arrival, vault transmission). Both must be purely environmental. No villain dialogue. No "I have been watching you." A system processing inputs. Red Eye communicates through behavior and architecture, not words. If these scenes tip into monologuing, they kill the tension that makes Skynet terrifying — the terror is that Skynet doesn't NEED to talk to you.

PROSE ECONOMY IN THE SCREENPLAY
* Some action paragraphs in the current draft run long. Screenplay prose should be lean — one image per sentence, one action per paragraph where possible. The reader's eye should move fast.
* Some dialogue could be tighter. Look for lines where characters explain what they're feeling instead of showing it. The bonding sequence is particularly vulnerable to this — the intimacy should live in behavior and silence, not articulation.
* The prologue especially needs a compression pass. Every word costs screen time the audience is spending with strangers.
